**What’s Driving High Electric Vehicle Fast Charging Prices?** (Job Market Paper)

*Abstract:* Fast charging stations are considered crucial to hastening electric vehicle adoption, but due to a lack of data, there has been little work on competition or consumer behavior. I compile novel datasets on charging station prices and charging quantities to measure how consumers and competing stations respond to new stations entering the market. I show that the fast charging market is characterized by high markups, but it is not obvious whether these reflect high fixed costs or firms' price-setting power. I implement a difference in differences model to measure the impacts of station entry and find no effect of market entry on competitor charging volumes or prices. Given the lack of price competition, I investigate how firms choose prices. I estimate a model of consumer demand for fast charging to recover own- and cross-price elasticities of demand. I use these elasticities to show that charging networks' prices are consistent with maximizing charging market profits with linear prices, which leads to higher prices and greater deadweight loss than two-part tariffs. Observed prices are higher than the prices that would most efficiently recover fixed costs, suggesting there is room for networks to lower prices.

**Takeoffs and Test Scores: The Impact of Noise Pollution on Standardized Test Performance** [link](/research/Noise_Pollution_draft.pdf)

*Abstract:* Noise pollution has become so ubiquitous in urban life that it is generally considered innocuous. However, research suggests that noise pollution can be more than a minor distraction and is associated with diminished health and productivity. I leverage exogenous shifts in noise pollution from changes in aircraft noise and use a value-added model that accounts for student ability and school quality to estimate the causal impact of airplane noise pollution on standardized test performance. I find a large negative impact on math test performance, but no impact on English language art test performance. Moving from a school at the 25<sup>th</sup> percentile of exposure in my dataset to one at the 75<sup>th</sup> percentile is associated with a math test score reduction of 0.10 standard deviations, twice as large as the impact of missing 10 school days during the year. Additionally, I find evidence that these negative impacts may be driven by the loudest days. These findings suggest a potentially large value to investing in noise insulation for schools exposed to high noise levels.
